A/B test design report for class preparation
Data Analytics | Case Brief

Which A/B test should Yelp use for the map-search advertising change?

Yelp should run a randomized two-group A/B test comparing the current ad-serving policy against a new map-boundary policy. The real decision is not just whether users click more. It is whether higher ad relevance is worth any loss in ad inventory or revenue.

Decision Snapshot

Use the test to decide whether the new policy improves ad quality without damaging Yelp's core advertising economics.

  • ControlExisting system: sponsored ads can appear outside the map area.
  • TreatmentNew system: sponsored ads must respect the user's map boundary.
  • PrimaryClickthrough rate on shown ads.
  • Must WatchRevenue per eligible search and ad shown rate.
1. Case Context

Why this experiment matters

Yelp connects users looking for local businesses with businesses that want to be discovered. Advertising is central to the business model. In 2019, advertising made up about 96% of Yelp's total net revenue, so even small changes in ad delivery can matter financially.

The case is about a managerial launch decision. The separate data analysis, if assigned in class, would be used to quantify the metric changes.
User side

Search relevance

Users searching inside a specific map area may expect results and sponsored results to stay inside that area.

Advertiser side

Paid discovery

Local businesses bid for sponsored placement when their ad matches the search opportunity.

Yelp side

Revenue and trust

Yelp wants relevant ads that users trust, but advertising revenue is too important to ignore.

2. Recommended Test

A randomized two-group A/B test

The cleanest design is to randomly assign eligible map-search traffic to either the old ad policy or the new map-boundary policy, then compare outcomes after enough data has been collected.

A: Control

Current ad-serving policy

Yelp can show sponsored results even when the advertised business is outside the visible map boundary. This preserves more ad inventory and may produce more monetizable ad slots.

B: Treatment

Map-boundary ad policy

Yelp filters sponsored results so the advertised business respects the user's selected map geography. This should improve perceived relevance, but may reduce available ads.

Population

  • Include users or sessions performing map searches that are eligible to show ads.
  • Exclude non-map searches, internal traffic, bots, and broken logging events.
->

Randomization

  • Prefer user-level or device-level assignment for a consistent experience.
  • Keep assignment stable during the test so a user does not bounce between policies.
3. Hypothesis

What Yelp is trying to learn

The team's hypothesis is that respecting the user's map boundary will make sponsored results feel more relevant, which should increase ad engagement.

The hypothesis must name both the product change and the metric expected to move. Otherwise, the team can tell a convenient story after the numbers arrive.

Business hypothesis

If Yelp restricts sponsored results to the map area, users will find the ads more useful and click at a higher rate.

Risk hypothesis

If Yelp restricts sponsored results too much, fewer ad opportunities will have eligible advertisers, and revenue per eligible search may fall.

4. Metric Logic

Why one good metric is not enough

The case is tricky because the primary engagement metric and the revenue metrics can move in different directions. The test should be interpreted through the full advertising funnel.

Revenue per eligible search ~= Ad shown rate x Clickthrough rate x Revenue per click

This relationship explains the managerial tension. The treatment can raise clickthrough rate while still lowering total revenue if it reduces ad shown rate or revenue per click.

1. Search occurs A user performs a map search, such as looking for bars or restaurants in a selected area.
2. Search is eligible Yelp decides whether this search can show an ad based on product and business rules.
3. Ad is shown Eligible advertisers compete in an auction; the winning sponsored result is displayed.
4. User clicks If the user clicks, Yelp earns ad revenue based on the auction price.
Count 1

Eligible for adverts

The number of map searches where Yelp is allowed to show a sponsored result. These are the opportunities in the denominator for monetization.

Count 2

Shown adverts

The number of eligible searches where Yelp actually finds and displays a sponsored result after applying matching, auction, and reserve-price rules.

Count 3

Clicked adverts

The number of shown sponsored results that users click. Clicks generate ad revenue and reveal whether shown adverts were attractive enough to act on.

5. Metrics

What to measure

The launch decision should be made from a pre-selected scorecard. The primary metric should indicate whether the user-facing improvement worked; the supporting metrics should reveal the business tradeoff.

Metric Formula Why it matters How to read it
Eligible searches count of searches allowed to show ads Defines the total opportunity pool for Yelp's advertising system. This is the denominator for ad shown rate and revenue per eligible search.
Clickthrough rate ad clicks / ads shown Primary ad-quality metric. It shows whether shown ads are more compelling or relevant. A lift supports the product hypothesis, but does not prove the change is financially attractive.
Ad shown rate ads shown / eligible searches Shows whether the stricter geographic rule reduces available ad inventory. A decline means users may see fewer ads, which can lower total revenue even if CTR rises.
Revenue per click ad revenue / ad clicks Captures the value of clicked ads, linked to advertiser bids and auction dynamics. A decline may mean the treatment shifts clicks toward lower-value advertisers.
Revenue per eligible search ad revenue / eligible searches Best single monetization metric for the launch decision. If this falls meaningfully, Yelp must decide whether long-term quality benefits justify the short-term revenue cost.
Guardrail metrics Predefined health checks Protects the wider Yelp ecosystem from hidden damage. Examples include user engagement, search satisfaction proxies, advertiser impact, latency, and logging health.
6. Validity Checks

How to make the test trustworthy

A/B tests are powerful only when the design and logging are clean. Yelp's Bunsen platform exists partly to standardize these details and avoid inconsistent metric definitions.

Pre-register the decision metric

Choose the primary metric and launch criteria before seeing the results.

Run a power analysis

Estimate the traffic and time needed to detect a meaningful change.

Check sample balance

Confirm that control and treatment groups have similar traffic and user mix.

Monitor guardrails

Stop or investigate if the treatment violates important business or technical thresholds.

Avoid early peeking

Do not call the result too early unless the experiment design allows interim decisions.

Segment carefully

Use segment analysis to learn, but do not cherry-pick segments to justify a preferred answer.

7. Interpretation

How the result can become surprising

The test can produce a messy answer because each metric describes a different part of the funnel. A simple "CTR went up" conclusion may hide a worse revenue picture.

CTR up, revenue flat
Strong launch candidate. The treatment improves apparent ad relevance without hurting monetization.
CTR up, revenue down
The central dilemma. Users click shown ads more often, but Yelp shows fewer ads or earns less per click. The team must judge whether long-term user trust is worth the short-term revenue cost.
CTR flat, revenue down
Do not launch. The treatment fails the quality hypothesis and weakens the business model.
CTR down, revenue up
Investigate carefully. The change may monetize better in the short run but make ads feel less relevant, which can harm the user experience over time.
Best class framing: Yelp is not deciding whether geography matters. It is deciding how much relevance it can require before the ad marketplace loses too much coverage or auction value.
8. Launch Framework

A practical recommendation rule

Use this framework to organize your answer in class. It keeps the discussion from becoming a single-metric debate.

  1. Confirm the experiment is valid. Randomization, logging, sample size, and guardrails must be credible.
  2. Start with the primary metric. Did the treatment improve clickthrough rate enough to matter?
  3. Translate the funnel into money. Did ad shown rate or revenue per click move in a way that changes revenue per eligible search?
  4. Separate short-term and long-term value. A small short-term revenue loss may be acceptable if the change protects user trust and ad quality.
  5. Choose one of three actions. Launch, do not launch, or iterate with a narrower rule such as applying the boundary only when enough qualified advertisers exist.
Recommended stance: Launch only if CTR improves and revenue per eligible search is neutral, positive, or within a pre-agreed acceptable loss. If CTR improves but revenue falls meaningfully, do not simply ship; run a follow-up design that balances map relevance with ad inventory.
9. Class Discussion Prompts

Questions to bring to class

These prompts help you sound managerial rather than purely technical.

  • Should clickthrough rate be the primary metric, or should revenue per eligible search be primary?
  • How much short-term revenue should Yelp be willing to sacrifice for better ad relevance?
  • Would you launch the treatment for all map searches, or only in markets with enough advertiser density?
  • What guardrail metric would make you stop the experiment immediately?
  • What follow-up experiment would you run if the result is directionally good but financially painful?
